Content and accuracy guide
How to make an article worth reading, keep it neutral, and stay out of legal trouble.
Add something new
This is the mistake we see most across our sites. If your article only retells the TikTok, it isn’t finished. The reader can watch the video. Your job is to give them something the video doesn’t. Only describing what’s in the video is against our rules and our partners’ rules.
Ways to add value:
* Context: what led up to this, and who the people or company are.
* Rules and laws: is what happened legal? What does the company’s policy say? (Link it.)
* The other side: did the company or person respond? Did you reach out?
* Follow-up questions: what would a curious reader want to know next?
Comments
What people said under the video.
* Use them rarely. A comment is seasoning, not the meal.
* Never write a whole section about comments.
* Each negative comment needs a positive one to balance it. 
* A good way to use one: mention a comment, then move into real reporting from it.
Tone: we report the drama, we don’t join it
Don’t write
	Why
	“Trump is definitely not picking a side”
	Sarcasm. Say what he said or did.
	“with her conscience conveniently muted by hot wings”
	A joke at someone’s expense.
	“the internet’s verdict was simple”
	Your opinion dressed up as fact.
	“Perhaps the next HOA meeting should have a metal detector”
	An imagined scenario. Not news.
	Also avoid: too many colons, “apparently,” saying the same thing twice, sounding stiff or robotic, and random bold text. Read your article out loud. If it doesn’t sound like a person talking, rewrite it.
Charged is not convicted
One wrong fact can get us sued or kicked off a partner. In the US, everyone is innocent until a court finds them guilty. Until then, we can only say what they’re accused of.
Stage
	What you can write
	Accused or investigated
	“John Doe is accused of…” / “police are investigating…”
	Arrested
	“John Doe was arrested in connection with…”
	Charged
	“John Doe was charged with second-degree assault” (use the exact charge)
	On trial
	“John Doe allegedly struck the man, prosecutors claim”
	Convicted
	“John Doe was found guilty of…” Now you can drop “allegedly.”
	Example:
* Wrong: “John Doe hit Jeffrey.”
* Right: “John Doe allegedly struck Jeffrey on the face, according to a police report.”
Rules to remember:
* Keep the hedging the whole way through the article, not just the first time.
* “Allegedly” goes on the suspect’s actions, never the victim’s.
* Police reports are claims too: write “a police report claims,” not “police revealed.”
* Never name a minor who is accused of a crime. 
* Mugshots and bodycam images are copyright-free. Government employees made them as part of their jobs, so we can use them, but only once the person has been officially charged with a crime. Never use one for someone who is only a suspect, detained, or arrested without charges, because police sometimes get the wrong person. Even then, nothing violent or graphic, and nothing that identifies a victim.
Facts and sources
* Every claim needs a link. Even “videos circulating on TikTok” needs one.
* Quotes must be exact. Check them against the video or article word for word. If you can’t confirm it, don’t use it as a quote. Quotes only come from the source, never from the comments.
* Small details matter. “Almost 50,000” and “more than 50,000” are different facts.
* Don’t leave out the other side. If someone responded, include it. If they didn’t respond, say so.
* Cite the original. If a news site is quoting a TikTok, link the TikTok, not the news site.
* Cite the full article, not a live blog or bulletin.
